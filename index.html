The impact of temperature exposure on intimate partner violence in Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (Sioux) and the surrounding Northern Plains states

Abstract

Abstract

Intimate partner violence (IPV) remains a persistent concern in tribal and surrounding communities across the Upper Midwest and Northern Great Plains. This is a region marked by extreme seasonal temperatures. Whether the association between temperature and IPV varies by offender race is unclear.

We linked seven years (2018–2024) of daily county-level reported IPV incidents across five states containing Očhéthi Šakówiŋ reservation to thermal exposure data. This was analyzed using negative binomial regression to compare American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN) and non-AI/AN offenders.

Reported IPV rose with extreme heat among non-AI/AN offenders but with extreme cold among AI/AN offenders. These are opposing patterns for the same climate stressor. Effect-modification tests confirmed these differences by offender race were statistically significant.

Abuse or aggression that occurs in a romantic relationship. “Intimate partner” refers to both current and former spouses and dating partners. Includes:

Theoretical framework

Adapted ecosocial theory

Ecosocial theory holds that health outcomes are the embodiment of social and structural conditions across the life course. It’s the framework this study uses to explain the pattern just described: the same climate stressor moves through layers of structural, community, and interpersonal conditions. Extreme weather intensifies vulnerability at every one of those layers, not just at the point of exposure.

Per this theory, AI/AN communities embody greater structural stress accumulated across the life course. The same thermal exposure therefore lands on a population with less adaptive capacity to absorb it.

Problem statement

Problem statement

Temperature-violence research has focused almost entirely on heat in cities, leaving cold-climate Indigenous communities essentially unstudied despite extreme seasonal swings and a disproportionate IPV burden. Without population-specific evidence, climate-violence-prevention policy assumes all populations have the same risk factors associated with temperature, not taking into consideration that some groups have higher structural vulnerability.

Hypothesis

Hypothesis

We postulated that temperature’s effect on reported IPV would be stronger among AI/AN offenders than non-AI/AN offenders, per the ecosocial reasoning above.

Behavioral & psychological mechanisms

Pathways & mechanisms

The literature proposes two competing mechanisms for each temperature extreme: one that theorizes IPV increases and another that suppresses IPV. Both extremes point toward the same outcome. Tap either side below to see the mechanisms and citations behind it.

Cold pathways 2 competing mechanisms, tap to explore +

↓ Cold decreases IPV

Behavioral suppression

  • Routine Activity Theory: less outdoor contact and opportunity (Cohen & Felson, 1979)
  • Physical deterrence: cold physically suppresses outdoor movement and encounter (Wolff & Thomas, 2023)

↑ Cold increases IPV

Structural stress

  • Confinement and chronic stress: heating failures force indoor co-presence (Anderson et al., 2000; Prudkov, 2019)
  • Economic stress, isolation, and alcohol: fuel and job losses drive isolation and coping via alcohol (Zhu & Andrade, 2025)
Heat pathways 2 competing mechanisms, tap to explore +

↑ Heat increases IPV

Primary

  • Neurobiological arousal: amygdala and hypothalamus activation (Plante et al., 2017; Anderson, 2002)
  • Routine Activity Theory: increased outdoor contact and co-presence (Cohen & Felson, 1979; Rotton & Cohn, 2001)

↓ Heat decreases IPV

Extreme heat only

  • Negative Affect Escape Hypothesis: withdraw rather than confront (Baron & Bell, 1976; Anderson, 2001)
  • Routine Activity Reversal: indoor retreat (Rotton & Cohn, 2001)

How we know

Defining extremes, modeling the signal

With the data in place, here’s exactly how “extreme” was defined and how the models isolated the temperature effect from everything else that varies by place and time.

Ten ways to define "extreme"

Heat and cold were each defined two ways: a fixed physical threshold, and a threshold relative to what's normal for that county. Both were tracked for single days and multi-day spells.

Every model controlled for the county itself, the calendar year, the day of the week, and normal seasonal patterns, using negative binomial regression with standard errors clustered at the county level. What's left is the temperature effect, not just "winter" or "a particular county having more reports."

The finding

Opposing patterns. Heat raised reported IPV for non-AI/AN offenders. Cold raised it for AI/AN offenders.

Full results

The full results, heat and cold side by side

The curve above summarizes the overall pattern. These are the underlying models it’s built from: every heat and cold threshold tested, across all three population groups.

Incidence rate ratios across all ten exposure definitions and three population groups. Error bars = 95% CI; larger filled points indicate p < 0.05. Negative binomial regression, daily county-level data, 2018–2024.

Heat showed no consistent association with reported IPV in any group. The one exception is a near-doubling of incidents on rare 5+ day heat waves (IRR 2.10, p=0.008, non-AI/AN and overall). That estimate rests on just 8 qualifying county-days across the entire seven-year study, so it's a fragile signal, not a confirmed effect.

Cold tells a clearer story. Single- and multi-day cold at or below 0°C associates with a consistent ~6% drop in reported IPV among non-AI/AN offenders and the overall population (p<0.001 for both). AI/AN-offender estimates sit close to 1.0 and trend in the opposite direction on most definitions. They're not statistically significant on their own, but they're different enough from the non-AI/AN pattern that the formal interaction test comes out significant (see Pathways & Mechanisms, above).

Heat exposure only, AI/AN vs. non-AI/AN offenders. Error bars = 95% CI. Study region: MN, MT, ND, NE, SD, 2018–2024.

Zooming in on heat alone shows just how imprecise the AI/AN estimates are, with wide confidence intervals crossing 1.0 throughout. The clearest heat signal in the whole study, the 5-day heat wave among non-AI/AN offenders, has no AI/AN counterpart at all: zero AI/AN-offender incidents occurred on any of the 8 qualifying county-days, so that model couldn’t be estimated.

Note for review: this figure’s own caption states the AI/AN estimate for Heat ≥30°C (≥3 days) is off-axis / non-convergent, but the paper’s Table 3 reports an estimable value for that same cell (IRR 4.08, 95% CI 1.19–14.03, p=0.026, based on very few incidents). Cold exposure only, AI/AN vs. non-AI/AN offenders. Error bars = 95% CI. Study region: MN, MT, ND, NE, SD, 2018–2024.

Zooming in on cold alone makes the divergence visible at a glance: non-AI/AN offenders sit consistently below 1.0 across all four cold definitions, with tight confidence intervals that barely touch the null line. AI/AN offenders sit at or above 1.0 on three of the four definitions, the opposite side of the line, though none of the individual AI/AN estimates reach significance on their own. It’s this consistent opposite-direction pattern, not any single AI/AN estimate, that drives the significant interaction test.

Ecosocial interpretation

The same stressor, cold, produces opposite associations depending on structural position. AI/AN communities in the Northern Plains carry compounding vulnerabilities (overcrowded housing, inadequate heating, high poverty, geographic isolation, historical trauma) that transform cold from a behavioral suppressor into a material stressor. This is the core prediction of ecosocial theory: environmental health associations are shaped by the structural conditions that determine adaptive capacity.

Limitations

What this study can’t tell us

Every result above should be read alongside these five constraints.

See all five limitations

NIBRS reporting bias

Captures only law-enforcement reports. AI/AN underreporting is likely, driven by jurisdictional complexity, historical policing distrust, and geographic distance. Cold may further suppress AI/AN reporting.

AI/AN heterogeneity

The AI/AN indicator doesn’t distinguish on- vs. off-reservation, or urban vs. rural. Aggregating diverse communities may mask heterogeneity in cold vulnerability.

County-level exposure

Thermal exposure is assigned county-wide, so within-county variation isn’t captured. Ecological fallacy applies: county-day patterns ≠ individual causal effects.

Unmeasured confounders

Time-varying factors, including substance use, seasonal (un)employment, and economic shocks, remain potential residual confounders.

Limited AI/AN statistical power

AI/AN offenders are 4.8% of the sample (n=6,959), which is insufficient power for rare exposures, especially absolute heat events. Null findings ≠ no effect.

Public health & policy implications

From finding to practice

Heating infrastructure

Weatherization, emergency fuel assistance, and backup heating on reservations offer co-benefits for violence prevention beyond direct health and economic effects.

Crisis resource deployment

Increase IPV helpline staffing and emergency shelter capacity during forecasted cold spells, analogous to existing heat-emergency protocols.

Disaggregated analysis

Population-average cold estimates could misdirect interventions. AI/AN-stratified analysis is essential for climate-health equity.

Climate adaptation planning

Federal, state, and tribal climate plans should incorporate IPV as an outcome domain, especially under projected increases in extreme weather.

Main takeaways

Main takeaway points

01

Cold protects, for non-AI/AN offenders

Cold exposure is consistently associated with reduced reported IPV among non-AI/AN populations in the Northern Plains, consistent with behavioral suppression theory.

02

The pattern reverses for AI/AN offenders

AI/AN offenders show a divergent pattern: cold estimates trend upward. Formal interaction tests confirm the cold–IPV association differs significantly by AI/AN status for absolute cold definitions (p < 0.001).

03

A first-of-its-kind finding

This is the first study to document a cold–IPV divergence by AI/AN status, consistent with ecosocial theory: structural vulnerabilities transform cold from a behavioral suppressor into a material stressor.

04

Heat’s signal is fragile

Heat associations were not robust. Rare 3- and 5-day absolute heat waves showed elevated IRRs, but rested on very few events and should be interpreted cautiously.

What’s next

Possible future directions

Open questions this study sets up, plus one commitment beyond the data itself.

  • 1Lagged effects. Implement 0–2 day lagged models to capture delayed thermal effects. Stress may precipitate IPV the day after peak exposure.
  • 2Severity, not just incidence. Investigate how the severity of IPV is impacted by temperature exposure, not just whether it occurs.
  • 3Future climate scenarios. Apply projected UTCI to estimate future hot- and cold-IPV burden shifts under climate change.
  • 4Community collaboration. Deepen collaboration with AI/AN communities and organizations affected by these findings, so future research reflects their priorities and questions rather than an outside agenda applied to them.
